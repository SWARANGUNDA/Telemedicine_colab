Dataset Overview
We will create a 20,000-patient synthetic clinical dataset  to train machine-learning models that predict
Type 2 Diabetes, Prediabetes, Obesity, Metabolic Syndrome, and NAFLD (non-alcoholic fatty liver disease) in
a telemedicine platform. The data should mimic a real hospital EHR: medically plausible, multi-label
(patients may have multiple conditions), and reflecting clinical diagnostic criteria. Using synthetic data
avoids patient privacy issues【31†L117-L125】 and allows full control over disease prevalence, feature
correlations, and missingness. It also enables reproducibility and easy adjustment of rules (for example,
adding new risk factors or changing prevalence) without requiring new IRB approvals【31†L117-L125】. 
Key goals are: ( 1) Medical realism:  follow evidence-based clinical guidelines for all lab values, vital signs,
and disease definitions【1†L449-L454】【9†L313-L323】. ( 2) Balanced case mix:  reflect a hospital-like
population (with significant rates of the target conditions) rather than a healthier community sample. ( 3) 
Machine-learning readiness:  ensure features have known ranges, controlled noise, and manage missing
data thoughtfully. We will document all assumptions so that the data can be automatically generated and
validated . 
Note:  Because all data are fully synthetic, there are no PHI concerns. We still apply standard
ethical considerations: avoid built-in biases (for example, not simulating race unless clinically
justified), use medically defensible rules, and document every design choice for transparency.
Disease Background
We target five metabolic-related conditions often managed in primary/specialty care. Each is described
below with its clinical features and risk factors.
Type 2 Diabetes (T2D):  A chronic disease of insulin resistance and pancreatic β-cell dysfunction.
Adults (often >45 years) develop hyperglycemia marked by high blood glucose and HbA1c【1†L449-
L454】【18†L163-L170】. Key risk factors include obesity/overweight, family history of diabetes,
sedentary lifestyle, and older age【18†L163-L170】. Major biomarkers are fasting plasma glucose
(FPG) and hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c)【1†L449-L454】【18†L163-L170】. T2D is important because it
leads to cardiovascular and renal complications; we include it to model glycemic dysregulation in the
patient population. 
Prediabetes:  A state of elevated blood sugar that is not yet in the diabetes range【1†L449-L454】. It
is an early warning of insulin resistance. Diagnostic criteria are FPG 100–125 mg/dL or HbA1c 5.7–
6.4%【1†L449-L454】【18†L163-L170】. Risk factors largely mirror T2D: overweight/obesity, family
history, inactivity, and NAFLD【18†L163-L170】. We include prediabetes because it often precedes
T2D and is common (tens of millions in the US【29†L438-L447】), and it can co-occur with other
metabolic conditions. •

Obesity:  A chronic condition of excess body fat, defined by BMI ≥30 kg/m²【10†L279-L282】.
Causes include diet/environment interactions, genetics, and certain medications【10†L251-L259】.
Obesity is a major risk factor for T2D, NAFLD, and Metabolic Syndrome. We follow WHO/CDC
definitions: overweight is BMI 25–29.9, obesity ≥30【3†L99-L107】【10†L279-L282】. It is clinically
important due to associated hypertension, dyslipidemia, and organ stress. We include obesity as a
disease label to capture patients whose BMI/waist values exceed these thresholds【3†L99-L107】
【9†L311-L319】. 
Metabolic Syndrome:  A cluster of metabolic risk factors that together greatly increase
cardiovascular risk. It is diagnosed when ≥3  of the following are present: large waist circumference
(visceral obesity), high triglycerides, low HDL, elevated blood pressure, and elevated fasting glucose
【5†L407-L416】【9†L313-L323】. Underlying causes include insulin resistance, obesity, inactivity,
age, and genetics【5†L424-L429】. We include it because it captures the interplay of multiple risk
factors. It often coexists with T2D and NAFLD, and signifies high cumulative risk. 
NAFLD (Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease):  Excess fat accumulation in the
liver (steatosis) not due to alcohol . It is defined by >5%–10% hepatic fat【15†L425-L430】. Risk
factors include obesity, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and diabetes【15†L455-L458】. Nearly all
NAFLD patients have at least one feature of Metabolic Syndrome. We include NAFLD as a label
because it is highly prevalent (~25% of adults【15†L445-L448】) and adds a liver-health dimension to
our predictions. There is no simple blood test for NAFLD, so we will assign it based on correlated
features (see Section 7). 
Each disease’s natural history influences feature values. For example, early-stage T2D  may only mildly
elevate fasting glucose with near-normal A1c, while severe T2D  often has FPG >>180 mg/dL and HbA1c
>10%. We will simulate mild, moderate, and severe cases by distributing values across clinically observed
ranges for each condition. 
Clinical Features
We will include the following variables. Each feature is chosen because it is routinely measured and relevant
to the target diseases.
Patient_ID: String.  A unique identifier (e.g. “P00001”). Unit:  none. Importance:  Ensures uniqueness. 
Mandatory:  Yes. Availability:  Always (by design). Validation:  Unique, non-null. 
Age: Integer (years).  Range ~18–85. Unit:  years. Clinical Role:  Risk factor for all conditions (older age
→ higher metabolic risk). Mandatory:  Yes. Availability:  Always known. Validation:  ≥18 (adult). 
Gender: Category.  Typically “Male” or “Female”. Mandatory:  Yes. Availability:  Always. Validation:
Must be one of the categories. (Sex-specific thresholds apply below.) 
Height_cm: Float (cm).  Adult height. Mandatory:  Yes. Availability:  Almost always measured. Clinical
Role:  Used to compute BMI; slight correlations with risk. Range:  ~145–190 cm (female) or 155–200 cm
(male). Validation:  Consistency check (weight vs. height for plausible BMI). •

Weight_kg: Float (kg).  Body weight. Mandatory:  Yes. Availability:  Almost always. Range:  ~40–150 kg.
Validation:  Check BMI bounds after computation. 
BMI: Float (kg/m²).  Body mass index. Computed:  from height and weight (BMI = kg/m²). Mandatory:
Yes (we compute it). Unit:  none. Range:  16–45 (covering underweight to class III obesity). 
Importance:  Direct measure for obesity label; strongly related to glucose, lipids, liver enzymes, and
blood pressure. Validation:  Must match Weight/Height (±rounding). 
Waist_Circumference_cm: Float (cm).  Abdominal girth. Mandatory:  No (often optional). 
Availability:  Often measured in metabolic/risk clinics; missing maybe 2–5%. Range:  ~60–130 cm
(women), 65–140 cm (men)【9†L349-L357】. Role:  Indicator of central obesity; used in Metabolic
Syndrome diagnosis. Validation:  Consistent with BMI (very high waist with low BMI should be rare). 
Systolic_BP: Integer (mmHg).  Systolic blood pressure. Mandatory:  Yes. Range:  ~90–190. Role:
Elevated BP is both a risk factor and a Metabolic Syndrome component【9†L324-L332】. Validation:
≥80 and ≥(Diastolic_BP + few mmHg). 
Diastolic_BP: Integer (mmHg).  Diastolic blood pressure. Mandatory:  Yes. Range:  ~60–120. Validation:
< Systolic_BP. 
Fasting_Blood_Glucose: Integer (mg/dL).  Fasting plasma glucose (before eating). Mandatory:  Yes. 
Range:  70–250. Role:  Primary screening measure for (pre)diabetes【1†L469-L472】. Validation:  ≥0;
FPG of ~70–99 normal, 100–125 prediabetes, ≥126 diabetes【1†L469-L472】. 
HbA1c: Float (%).  Glycated hemoglobin (2–3 month average glucose). Mandatory:  Yes. Range:  4.5–
12.0%. Role:  Diabetes screening/monitoring【1†L449-L454】. Validation:  4.5–5.6 normal, 5.7–6.4
prediabetes, ≥6.5 diabetes【1†L449-L454】. 
LDL_Chol: Integer (mg/dL).  LDL cholesterol (“bad” chol). Mandatory:  No (often optional in outpatient
settings). Availability:  Common in lipid panels; missing ~2–5%. Range:  40–250. Interpretation:
Optimal <100, near-optimal 100–129【25†L154-L163】. High LDL is a CVD risk factor often co-
occurring with metabolic syndrome. Validation:  ≥0. 
HDL_Chol: Integer (mg/dL).  HDL cholesterol (“good” chol). Mandatory:  No. Availability:  Same as LDL.
Range:  20–90. Interpretation:  Men ≥40, women ≥50 are considered normal【25†L160-L163】; lower
levels contribute to MetS. Validation:  ≥0. 
Triglycerides: Integer (mg/dL).  Blood triglycerides. Mandatory:  No. Range:  40–500. Interpretation:
Normal <150【25†L163-L166】; 150–199 borderline, 200–499 high; high TG is a MetS criterion
【9†L313-L320】. Validation:  ≥0. 
ALT: Integer (U/L).  Alanine transaminase, a liver enzyme. Mandatory:  No. Availability:  Often ordered
if liver disease is suspected. Range:  10–200. Normal:  ~7–55【23†L462-L470】. Elevated ALT often
indicates NAFLD or other liver injury. Validation:  ≥0. 
AST: Integer (U/L).  Aspartate transaminase. Mandatory:  No. Range:  10–150. Normal:  ~8–48
【23†L462-L470】. Elevated AST can accompany ALT in NAFLD. Validation:  ≥0. •

Height:  Adult human range; we allow ∼140–210 cm. (For a 5th–95th percentile, ~150–200 cm.) 
Weight:  Allow 40–150 kg. (Below 40 kg is extreme adult underweight; above 150 kg is severe obesity.)

Waist Circumference: Thresholds:  Elevated risk starts at ≥94 cm (men) and ≥80 cm (women);

Systolic BP: Normal:  <120 mmHg【35†L229-L237】. Elevated:  120–129. Stage 1 Hypertension:  130–139.

Population Design
Age/Gender:  We simulate an adult clinic population (18–85). We use the specified age distribution: roughly
20% aged 18–30, 30% in 31–45, 30% in 46–60, 20% in 61–85. Gender is balanced ~50/50. (Older age and
equal gender produce a realistic mix.) 
BMI Distribution:  Obesity is common. We target ~24% obese (BMI≥30) and a higher percentage
overweight (25–29.9), reflecting global trends (WHO: ~43% overweight, 16% obesity worldwide【10†L314-
L322】). This yields a mean BMI around 27–30, with some values above 40 (class III obesity). 
Blood Pressure:  Given age and obesity, we expect many pre-hypertensive/hypertensive patients. We might
simulate ~30–40% with SBP ≥130 or on meds, matching guidelines that ~45% of U.S. adults have “high BP”
under the new definition【35†L229-L237】. 
Glucose/Lipids:  Corresponding to the BMI distribution, a substantial fraction will have elevated glucose or
dyslipidemia. For example, ~15% may have undiagnosed diabetes-level fasting glucose and ~20–30%
prediabetes. We will inject ~10–20% with LDL >130 mg/dL and similar with low HDL, consistent with
metabolic risk. 
Liver Enzymes:  Most patients will have normal ALT/AST; we plan ~2–5% with mild elevation (possible
NAFLD) and ~1–2% with moderate/severe elevation. 
Dataset Size:  We use 20,000 patients (as requested). This is large enough for stable model training and
capturing tail patterns. (Academic synthetic-data studies often use similar sizes【31†L117-L125】.) 
Justification:  The chosen distributions mirror known population statistics (e.g., 12% adult diabetes, 35%
prediabetes in the US【29†L438-L447】; 43% overweight globally【10†L314-L322】) but are tuned to the
requested prevalence in Section 9. The mix reflects a clinically realistic sampling rather than a healthy
volunteer sample.•

Feature Relationships
We will enforce medically valid correlations between features:
BMI & Waist Circumference: Very strong positive . By definition, higher BMI (due to weight)
implies larger waist. We will make them nearly deterministic (e.g. waist = 0.5×BMI*2.5 with noise) so
BMI and waist track closely. 
BMI & Blood Pressure: Moderate positive . Higher BMI increases risk of hypertension. We will
model SBP/DBP increasing somewhat with BMI (e.g. +1–2 mmHg per BMI point above 25). 
BMI & Glucose/HbA1c: Moderate positive.  Obesity promotes insulin resistance, so fasting glucose
and HbA1c tend to rise with BMI. For example, among high-BMI patients, a larger fraction will have
prediabetic or diabetic glucose levels【18†L163-L170】. 
BMI & Triglycerides (TG): Moderate positive.  Obesity/diet can raise TG. We will correlate TG with
BMI modestly. 
BMI & HDL: Moderate negative.  Higher BMI often associates with lower HDL. We will ensure HDL
tends to be lower in high-BMI individuals. 
Age & Risk Factors: Positive.  Older patients are more likely to have hypertension, diabetes, and
Metabolic Syndrome. We will trend SBP, FPG, and disease risk upward with age. 
Fasting Glucose & HbA1c: Strong positive.  Both measure glycemia. We will ensure consistent
labeling: if FPG is in the diabetic range, HbA1c should also generally be high. 
Triglycerides & HDL: Inverse moderate.  Dyslipidemia often presents with high TG and low HDL
simultaneously. 
Waist & Metabolic Risk:  Larger waist correlates with all MetS factors. We’ll often only label MetS=1 if
waist is high or several other factors are high. 
Diabetes & NAFLD: Positive correlation.  NAFLD frequently co-occurs with diabetes (as they share
pathophysiology【15†L455-L458】【18†L163-L170】). We will increase NAFLD probability in patients
with obesity, high TG, and/or diabetes. 
Other:  Systolic and diastolic BP correlate strongly (we ensure SBP > DBP). Male/female differences:
e.g., women tend to have higher HDL on average【25†L160-L163】, so we’ll add a slight gender
effect on HDL. 
These relationships ensure that the synthetic population follows known medical patterns: e.g. obese
patients tend to be hypertensive and dyslipidemic, diabetic patients tend to have metabolic syndrome
features, etc【18†L163-L170】【15†L455-L458】.
Disease Assignment Strategy
Each disease label will be assigned based on multiple features, following clinical criteria with some
randomness to simulate variability. We use multi-factor rules  rather than single cutoffs:
Type 2 Diabetes:  Assign if Fasting Glucose ≥126 mg/dL OR HbA1c ≥6.5% 【1†L449-L454】
【1†L469-L472】. In practice, we might not require both, but a patient meeting either threshold will
be labeled diabetic. (If both FPG and A1c are borderline high, we may use probability or secondary
criteria.) We ensure patients labeled T2D usually have other risk factors (e.g. BMI≥30, high TG,
hypertension) to reflect severity. •

Prediabetes:  Assign if (100 ≤ Fasting Glucose ≤ 125) OR (5.7% ≤ HbA1c ≤ 6.4%) , and NOT already
labeled T2D 【1†L449-L454】【1†L469-L472】. Prediabetic patients should have mildly elevated
glucose/A1c without reaching diabetic thresholds. We’ll also simulate that some with borderline lab
values but fewer risk factors get Prediabetes. 
Obesity:  Assign if BMI ≥ 30  (or equivalently waist ≥102 cm men / ≥88 cm women)【3†L111-L119】
【9†L349-L357】. We generate Class I/II/III obesity labels depending on BMI (e.g. 30–34.9, 35–39.9,
≥40) but the main binary label is 1 for any BMI≥30. (This follows WHO/CDC standards【3†L111-
L119】【10†L279-L282】.) 
Metabolic Syndrome:  Assign MetS=1 if any 3 or more  of the following criteria are met【5†L407-
L416】【9†L313-L323】: (1) Waist circumference above threshold (men ≥102 cm or women ≥88 cm)
【9†L349-L357】; (2) Triglycerides ≥150 mg/dL【9†L313-L317】; (3) HDL <40 mg/dL (men) or
<50 mg/dL (women)【9†L317-L323】; (4) BP ≥130/85 mmHg【9†L324-L329】; (5) Fasting glucose
≥100 mg/dL【9†L329-L334】. These are the ATP III/AHA/NHLBI criteria【9†L313-L323】. We will
ensure at least 3 factors to label MetS. (For example, a patient with central obesity, high TG, and high
BP would qualify.) 
NAFLD (MASLD):  Since we don’t have imaging, we assign NAFLD probabilistically based on risk
factors. Patients with obesity  (high BMI), hypertriglyceridemia , elevated ALT/AST , or existing 
diabetes  get higher NAFLD probability. For example, if BMI>28 and TG>180 mg/dL and ALT>50 U/L,
label NAFLD=1 with high probability. We also ensure patients with both obesity and T2D often have
NAFLD【15†L455-L458】【18†L163-L170】. Conversely, lean patients with normal labs will rarely get
NAFLD. (In effect, NAFLD depends on a weighted sum of factors: obesity, dyslipidemia,
transaminases, diabetes, age.) 
Healthy:  Assign Healthy=1 only if all five disease labels = 0 . Otherwise Healthy=0. This ensures non-
overlap (e.g., if any disease is present, Healthy must be 0). 
We implement these rules in code so that each synthetic patient’s lab/vital values jointly determine the
labels. For realism, we add randomness: e.g. a few cases might have BMI 32 but not labeled obesity
(measurement error), or borderline MetS factors that randomly count. This avoids deterministic, step-
function data.
Disease Co-occurrence
Real patients often have multiple conditions concurrently. Our synthetic data will reflect known co-
occurrences:
Diabetes and Obesity:  Very common. Most T2D patients will also have obesity (or at least
overweight). We will simulate that ~70–80% of labeled diabetics also have BMI>30. (This mirrors the
fact that obesity is the strongest risk factor for T2D【18†L163-L170】.) 
Diabetes and Metabolic Syndrome:  Almost all T2D patients meet MetS criteria (type 2 diabetes
itself would add one criterion)【9†L329-L334】. We will ensure most diabetics also trigger MetS. •

Obesity and MetS:  Overlap is high. Many obese individuals have high TG, low HDL, or hypertension.
We will make sure a large fraction of BMI≥30 patients are labeled MetS=1. 
NAFLD with MetS/Diabetes:  NAFLD almost never occurs in isolation; it strongly overlaps with MetS
components【15†L455-L458】. We will have NAFLD positive patients usually also meet MetS criteria
or have T2D. (For instance, >80% of NAFLD cases will coincide with obesity or elevated fasting
glucose.) 
Prediabetes with MetS:  Common. Many prediabetics will also have dyslipidemia or hypertension.
We will allow overlap: a patient can be both Prediabetes=1 and MetS=1 if they meet both rule sets. 
These co-occurrences will not be 100% deterministic but will follow probabilities. We can cite that metabolic
risk factors tend to cluster: e.g., NAFLD “tends to develop in people who have increased body fat or have
diabetes, high cholesterol, or high triglycerides; having a combination of these is known as metabolic
syndrome”【15†L455-L458】. In short, comorbidities will naturally cluster . 
Disease Prevalence
We aim for these approximate prevalences in the synthetic dataset  (note: these are higher than general-
population rates, reflecting a clinical population):
Healthy (no diseases): ~30%.  (Majority have at least one metabolic condition.) 
Prediabetes:  ~18%. (By comparison, in the U.S. ~30–35% of adults have prediabetes【29†L438-
L447】; we target a somewhat lower figure since we deliberately include many chronic cases.) 
Type 2 Diabetes:  ~16%. (In the U.S., ~12% of adults have diagnosed diabetes【29†L438-L447】. We
use ~16% to represent a clinic population with more risk factors.) 
Obesity:  ~24%. (We target ~24% with BMI≥30. Globally ~16% of adults are obese【10†L314-L322】;
prevalence is higher in some countries and in health-care settings.) 
Metabolic Syndrome:  ~18%. (Literature reports varying prevalence (e.g. 14–39% in different
countries【26†L6-L10】). Around 20% is reasonable for a synthetic outpatient mix.) 
NAFLD:  ~12%. (General estimates say ~25% of people have NAFLD【15†L445-L448】; we use a lower
12% to avoid dominating the dataset, while still allowing NAFLD cases.) 
These targets are achieved by adjusting the rules and random sampling. We cite authoritative stats for
context: e.g., ADA reports ~12% diabetes and 35% prediabetes in U.S. adults【29†L438-L447】, WHO/CDC
report ~43% overweight and 16% obesity in adults【10†L314-L322】, and liver associations estimate ~25%
NAFLD prevalence【15†L445-L448】. Our synthetic prevalences are approximate  and may slightly fluctuate,
but we will validate after generation (Section 14) to ensure they are in the desired range.

creates “edge” cases for robustness. Examples of borderline values (and few cases around them) include:

HbA1c:  values like 5.8–6.5% (prediabetes/diabetes boundary). 
Fasting Glucose:  e.g. 124–127 mg/dL (just below/above 126). 
Blood Pressure:  e.g. SBP 128–132 mmHg and DBP 84–86 mmHg (around the 130/85 cutoff). •

Triglycerides:  e.g. 145–152 mg/dL (near 150). 
HDL:  e.g. 39–42 mg/dL (men, cutoff 40). 
Waist Circumference:  e.g. 100–104 cm (male) or 86–90 cm (female) (around MetS criteria).

Including these borderline patients helps train models to distinguish mild from moderate cases. It also tests
that the label assignment logic handles exact-threshold values correctly. In practice, medical decisions often
hinge on such borderline labs, so modeling them improves real-world utility. (In contrast, random
assignment might yield fewer such cases.) 
Noise Model
To mimic real clinical measurements, we will add small random variability to numeric values:
Height:  ±0.5 cm (measurement error or rounding). 
Weight:  ±1 kg (scale variation). 
BMI:  recomputed from noisy height/weight. 
Blood Pressure:  ±3 mmHg (typical cuff error) for both SBP and DBP. 
HbA1c:  ±0.2%. 
Fasting Glucose:  ±5 mg/dL. 
LDL:  ±5 mg/dL. 
HDL:  ±3 mg/dL. 
Triglycerides:  ±8 mg/dL. 
ALT/AST:  ±3 U/L. 
These perturbations are small (on the order of normal laboratory or measurement error) so they break
exact ties  but do not blur disease definitions. We will ensure no value goes outside physiologically possible
limits after adding noise. Excessive noise is avoided because clinical measurements are relatively precise;
we only want to reflect typical lab/imaging variability. 
Outlier Strategy
To capture rare extreme cases, we will include a small number (~2%) of outliers with severe but plausible
values. Examples of extreme yet valid cases:
Extreme obesity:  BMI ~40–45, weight > 120 kg. 
Severe dyslipidemia:  Triglycerides 300–500 mg/dL, LDL >200 mg/dL. 
Poorly controlled diabetes:  HbA1c 10–12%, Fasting Glucose >200 mg/dL. 
Elevated liver enzymes:  ALT ~150–180 U/L, AST ~100–130 U/L. 
Combined crises:  e.g. BMI 43 with HbA1c 10.5% and TG 400. 
These cases should remain physiologically plausible. For instance, we would never generate ALT=1000 or
LDL=500 (those exceed human limits). We also avoid contradictory outliers (e.g. BMI 18 with 150 kg weight).
Outliers increase the model’s robustness to rare presentations, but we keep their proportion low.•

Missing Data Strategy
In real clinical data, not every feature is measured for every patient. We classify features by expected
missingness:
Always Available (0% missing):  Patient_ID, Age, Gender , Height, Weight, BMI, SBP, DBP,
Fasting_Glucose, HbA1c, and all disease labels . These are mandatory in our generated dataset so
the model always has core inputs and labels. 
Usually Available (<5% missing):  e.g. Waist_Circumference and Family_History fields. We simulate a
small fraction (1–5%) of these as missing (NULL) to reflect that some clinics may omit waist
measurements or patients may not know family history. 
Occasionally Missing (5–10%):  Liver enzymes (ALT, AST) may be missing in many routine visits.
Family_History_NAFLD may be missing up to ~8–10%, since patients often don’t know if relatives had
fatty liver . We will implement: Waist ~3% missing; LDL, HDL, TG ~2–3% missing; ALT, AST ~5%; Family
histories ~5–8%. 
Missingness will not be purely random . We will induce realistic patterns: for example, younger healthy
patients are less likely  to have extensive labs (so we’ll make missing lipid tests more likely if BMI<25 and
age<30). Conversely, a patient with obesity/diabetes is more likely to have a full lipid panel. Family history
fields will be missing at random or based on age/family size (e.g. older patients more likely to know family
history of hypertension). 
We will record missing values explicitly as NULL and ensure the model can impute or ignore them. (In
practice, one could use median imputation or a learned imputer on training data.) Documenting
missingness patterns is important for the ML pipeline to handle them appropriately.
Data Validation Rules
After generating each patient, we apply strict validation to catch errors:
Value Ranges:  Every numeric value must lie within the specified realistic bounds (see Section 4). E.g.
70≤FPG≤250, 4.5≤HbA1c≤12, 0≤LDL<300, 0≤AST<200, etc. Negative or physiologically
impossible values are rejected. 
BMI Consistency:  Compute BMI from height and weight; it must match the stored BMI (within
rounding). If not, we correct or regenerate the record. 
Blood Pressure:  Ensure SBP ≥ DBP and both are >=0. Also reject SBP<50 or DBP<30, which are
incompatible with life. 
Diabetes Labels:  If T2D=1, verify the patient has qualifying labs (FPG≥126 or HbA1c≥6.5)【1†L449-
L454】【1†L469-L472】. If not, fix the label. Similarly, Prediabetes=1 requires FPG 100–125 or HbA1c
5.7–6.4; if the labs are inconsistent, relabel or adjust labs. (We ensure no patient is marked diabetic
without lab evidence.) •

Metabolic Syndrome Label:  If Metabolic_Syndrome=1, check that the patient has at least 3 of the
defining criteria【5†L407-L416】【9†L313-L323】. If fewer , we remove the label. Conversely, if 3+
criteria are met, ensure MetS=1. 
Obesity Label:  If Obesity=1, ensure BMI≥30 or waist meets the cutoffs【3†L111-L119】【9†L349-
L357】. If not, correct it. 
NAFLD Label:  Since this is probabilistic, we still verify that NAFLD cases have elevated liver enzymes
or severe metabolic features; if a NAFLD label appears for a perfectly healthy profile, it will be
flagged for review. 
Healthy Label:  Enforce Healthy=1 only when all other disease labels are 0. If any disease is 1,
Healthy must be 0. 
Duplicates:  Patient_ID must be unique. We check for duplicate rows and reject or reseed as needed. 
Cross-checks:  We prevent impossible combos, e.g. BMI=18 with Weight=150 kg (contradiction), or
HDL>LDL (while possible, it’s unusual if values are normal). If an inconsistency is found (e.g. Weight
and Height give BMI 23 but the record lists BMI 35), we either correct it or regenerate. 
Missing Label Leakage:  No feature should be directly determined by a label. For example, we must
ensure HbA1c or FPG values do not “see” the disease label  – we generate features first, then
labels from them, so there is no leakage of the label into the feature values. 
All these rules will be coded into the generator’s validation step. Any patient failing a rule is either corrected
(if easy) or discarded and re-sampled. This ensures the final CSV has no obvious data errors. 
Machine Learning Considerations
We prepare the dataset with standard ML best practices:
Encoding:  Gender will be binary-coded (e.g. 0/1). All other features are numeric. Disease labels are
binary. No additional categorical variables. 
Scaling:  Since tree-based models (Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM) are planned, scaling is not
strictly necessary. If we also use logistic regression or neural nets, we could normalize continuous
features (e.g. zero-mean/unit-variance). But in a tree ensemble, raw scales (mmHg, mg/dL, etc.) are
acceptable. 
Missing Values:  For model training, missing numeric fields (waist, lipids, ALT/AST) could be imputed
(e.g. with the median or mean of training data) or a missing indicator could be added. We will handle
missingness carefully – for example, imputing NAFLD risk factors as normal if unknown. Categorical
missingness (none in our design except family history flags) could be filled with 0/“None”. Any
imputation should be fit on the training set only  and then applied to validation/test. 
Feature Engineering:  We already compute BMI from height/weight. We could also create features
like “Waist/BMI”, pulse pressure (SBP–DBP), or interactions, but we won’t overcomplicate. The chosen
raw features themselves (especially lab values and vitals) should suffice for gradient-boosted trees. 
Class Imbalance:  Some labels (e.g. NAFLD at 12%) are imbalanced. We can use techniques like class
weighting or oversampling positive cases in training for those diseases. Evaluation metrics (below)
will account for imbalance. 
Validation:  Use stratified K-fold cross-validation, ensuring each fold has similar disease prevalence.
We also reserve a final test set (e.g. 20%) for unbiased evaluation. 
Evaluation Metrics:  For each disease label (multi-label setting), we will report sensitivity, specificity,
precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC. Overall accuracy is less informative due to imbalance;
macro-averaged F1 is better . Precision-recall curves can be used for rare conditions. •
